A Century Chasing the Sun

From a hunch in 1920 to energy records and billions of dollars in investment: the history of nuclear fusion in 7 moments.

Every second, the Sun turns about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium. No smokestacks, no long-lived waste, just light. For a century, humanity has been trying to do the same thing here on Earth.

This is the story of that quest: visionary scientists, Cold War secrets, a summit between Reagan and Gorbachev and, today, an industrial race with billions of dollars at stake.

1The hunch: where does the Sun get its energy?

In 1920, British astrophysicist Arthur Eddington put forward a bold idea: stars shine because they fuse hydrogen nuclei into helium. In the process, a small fraction of the mass disappears and turns into energy, just as Einstein's famous equation, E = mc², predicts.

In 1934, Oliphant, Harteck and Rutherford fused deuterium (a "heavy hydrogen") in a laboratory for the first time. The physics worked. The hard part would be making it useful.

Analogy

Fusing two nuclei is like trying to make two magnets touch when they repel each other with enormous force. You have to hurl them at each other at tremendous speeds, and that means temperatures of more than 100 million degrees, several times hotter than the center of the Sun.

2The Cold War: from secrecy to cooperation

Fusion entered the world through its darkest door. On November 1, 1952, the United States detonated the first hydrogen bomb, hundreds of times more powerful than the one dropped on Hiroshima. The Soviet Union answered in less than a year.

Meanwhile, both blocs were secretly researching how to tame that energy. The first attempts failed: the plasma (the ultra-hot gas where fusion happens) escaped from every magnetic cage.

In 1955, Homi Bhabha, president of the "Atoms for Peace" conference, predicted that controlled fusion would arrive "within the next two decades". That gave birth to the field's most famous joke: fusion is always 20 or 30 years away.

In 1958, in Geneva, the United States, the USSR and the United Kingdom declassified their research. A total of 111 scientific papers were presented in an atmosphere of openness unheard of at the time.

Key fact

Fusion is one of the few nuclear technologies that geopolitical rivals have developed together for almost 70 years. A fusion reactor does not produce weapons material, and that made cooperation easier.

31968: the Soviet tokamak that won over the world

In Moscow, the Kurchatov Institute developed a doughnut-shaped machine called a tokamak (a Russian acronym for "toroidal chamber with magnetic coils"). In 1968, its T-3 tokamak reached temperatures of about 10 million degrees, far above anything the West had achieved.

Nobody believed them. So, at the height of the Cold War, a British team traveled to Moscow with a laser system to measure the temperature independently. The Soviet results were confirmed. Within two years, laboratories in the U.S., Europe and Japan had designed their own tokamaks.

Analogy

A tokamak is an invisible bottle made of magnets. Since no material can withstand 100 million degrees, the plasma floats inside a magnetic doughnut without touching the walls. It's like holding hot jelly using only rubber bands, without spilling a drop.

4The giants: JET and ITER

In 1985, at the Geneva Summit, Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev proposed building an international experimental reactor together: ITER. Today it is being built in France by seven partners (the European Union, China, India, Japan, Korea, Russia and the United States) representing more than 30 countries.

Meanwhile, the European tokamak JET, in England, became the record champion. Its farewell was epic: in 2023 it released 69 megajoules in 5 seconds using just 0.2 milligrams of fuel.

Analogy

0.2 milligrams is about a hundred times less than a grain of rice. But note: 69 MJ is only enough to heat the water for a few bathtubs, and JET spent more energy heating the plasma than it produced. It was a scientific record, not a power plant.

ITER, for its part, is the classic example of a difficult megaproject. In 2024 it announced a new schedule: research operations in 2034 and the use of real fusion fuel (deuterium-tritium) in 2039, about 4 years later than planned, at an additional cost of about 5 billion euros.

5December 2022: ignition

The other big path is lasers. At the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in California, 192 laser beams compress a fuel capsule the size of a peppercorn. On December 5, 2022, for the first time in history, fusion released more energy (3.15 MJ) than the laser delivered to the target (2.05 MJ).

By June 2026, NIF had achieved ignition 11 times. Its best shot, in April 2025, produced 8.6 MJ, four times the laser energy.

Analogy

NIF wins at the table but loses at the casino. The capsule returns 4 times what it receives, but powering the lasers takes about 300 MJ from the grid. It proves the physics works, not that the business works.

6The arrival of private money

For 70 years, fusion was a matter for governments. That changed this decade. According to the Fusion Industry Association, cumulative investment in fusion companies grew from $1.9 billion in 2021 to $14.2 billion in 2026, with a record $4.5 billion in the last year alone.

Today there are 56 companies in the sector and more than 16,000 employees. Tech giants are among the investors: in 2025, one of the world's largest technology companies signed a contract to buy electricity from a future tokamak plant with high-temperature superconducting magnets, to be built in Virginia, in the United States. The energy demand of artificial intelligence has turned data centers into the first potential customers.

The other side

The companies themselves estimate they need about $77 billion to reach commercialization, roughly 8 times what had been invested through 2025. 83% say that raising money is still their biggest challenge.

7Why this time could be different

The "always 30 years away" joke has an explanation: for decades, fusion received far less money than its own plans called for. Today, three new factors are coming together:

High-temperature superconducting magnets

Stronger fields in less space: smaller, cheaper reactors.

Supercomputing and artificial intelligence

They simulate and control the plasma in real time.

Private capital and real customers

Power purchase agreements already signed.

Nothing guarantees success. But for the first time in a hundred years, the question is no longer just whether the physics works, but who will make it work as a business.

In summary

Fusion went from an astrophysics idea, to a military secret, to a diplomatic project and, today, to an industry. Each stage left a lesson: science is necessary, but politics and economics set the pace.
